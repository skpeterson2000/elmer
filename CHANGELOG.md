# Changelog

One line a commit, by day, newest first - the subject line of each commit,
which in this repository is written as a sentence about what changed for
the person using it. `git log` has the rest. The build a unit runs is the
short commit id on its dashboard and in every problem report.

## 2026-09-19

- Click the ELMER icon in the corner and it keys its name again. The opening announcement went out once and could not be asked for a second time, which is a plain thing to want - to check the sound, to show somebody, to hear it. The icon does it now and stays on the page; the wordmark beside it is still the way home, and so is Dashboard in the bar. A second click restarts it rather than keying two names at once. It fits the machinery rather than fighting it: a browser keeps audio silent until the page has been touched, and a click on the icon is that touch. With the announcement switched off in the Station panel the icon is a plain link and keys nothing - the room where the announcement is unwelcome is exactly the room where a stray click must not sound
- "Free for everyone" said more than the licence does, and it said it in nine places. ELMER is under PolyForm Noncommercial 1.0.0, which the README and DESIGN.md state correctly - free for personal study, clubs, schools and other noncommercial use, and deliberately not an OSI open-source licence - while the Station panel, the Software panel, the coffee card, the hall's thanks card, the user guide, SUPPORTERS.md and DESIGN.md's own coffee section all said "free for everyone". A commercial user is part of everyone, and for them it is not free: they are not charged, they are not licensed. Every one of those sentences now uses the licence's own words, free for noncommercial use, and keeps the point it was making - that a cup of coffee is thanks and not a toll
- Where the station stands is no longer sealed with the password, and every answer built on it works again from a cold start. The QTH went on the sealed list two days ago alongside the RepeaterBook token, which looked like caution and was not: the key that opens sealed data lives in the running process and dies with it, so every single start left ELMER unable to say where it was standing until somebody typed a password. Everything downstream went quiet at once - no path to anywhere, no sky over the station, HF missing from Make Contact, **Locate me** refused outright - and it went quiet politely, answering "I do not know where you are" with no error anywhere, which is why it read as the program breaking rather than as a lock. A grid square is on every card that ever left the shack; a token for somebody else's service is a real credential and stays sealed. An account that already has its QTH under lock gets it back, in the clear and for good, the first time its owner signs in
- A distance on a screen is in the units the operator chose, on every screen. The preference existed and was right, and it reached exactly one page: everywhere else a unit was picked and written into the sentence, so the same skip zone came out in kilometres on the Lab page and in miles on the band plan, and whichever one an operator had chosen they were being overruled somewhere. Skip distance was the case that prompted this and was also listed, wrongly, as a thing that stays in kilometres because that is what ionosondes report - but the height of the F2 layer is a property of the layer, while a skip zone is how far away the nearest person who can hear you is standing, which is the same question as how far the next park is. The layer is still 300 km up whatever you drive in, a band is still named in metres and wire is still cut in feet
- A town on the compass is scored at the angle that actually reaches it, not along the ground. Every place was asked what the antenna does at nought degrees of elevation, so a town off the end of the wire came out sixty decibels down and was drawn in red as unworkable - Duluth called unreachable from a hundred miles off because the wire happened to point at it. A place a hundred miles away is reached by a ray leaving at about seventy five degrees, and at seventy five degrees a wire has no null: it is half a decibel from round. Reciprocity makes the listening case the same case, because the signal coming back arrives at the angle it would have left at, and an antenna has one pattern for both. What has not changed is line of sight: a repeater really is worked along the ground, and there the end null is real, so those are still scored at nought
- The plan view draws both slices of the pattern, and stops calling a round antenna deaf. A 30 ft inverted V on 80 m was drawn as a pinched figure-of-eight and described, flatly, as "deaf toward 90 E and 270 W". Both were true of the slice being drawn and false of the antenna: that slice is cut along the ground, and a wire radiating from about a sixteenth of a wavelength up puts almost nothing along the ground. At the angle it actually works at - straight up, for that antenna - it is within half a decibel of the same in every direction. The deep nulls everybody knows a dipole has fill in as the takeoff angle rises, and by the zenith there is no direction left at all. The filled shape is now the slice at the antenna's own main lobe, the ground slice sits behind it as a dashed outline where the two differ, and the words name the angle and say how many decibels down the weakest direction really is rather than reaching for an absolute. A beam's two slices coincide, so it draws one trace and no legend
- The inverted V's plan view had no inverted V in it. field_at(), which draws the plan view, reads the DX bearing table and prints the sheet, returned the plain dipole shape for every wire, while field_toward() - the one consulted when ELMER works out whether you can actually hear somebody - had the V half way to round because its legs slope. One antenna, two models, disagreeing on the same page: the same fault as the golf card, and as the V's own elevation pattern, which was corrected without anybody noticing the plan view had it too. There is one statement of it now, and the two agree exactly along the ground
- The band tag under a frequency box names the band the box is actually holding. Reported from a screenshot: 3.695 MHz in the antenna calculator with "20 m" written under it. Nothing was wrong with the antenna - it was cut for 80 m and its elevation pattern drawn for 80 m, and the band table names 3.695 as 80 m at every layer that was asked - but typing in the box is what repaints the tag, and ELMER setting the box itself, which is what **suggest one** does and what every carry-over between panes does, changed the value without telling anything. So the tag went on naming the band of whatever was there before. One number with two renderings allowed to disagree, which is the golf card and the inverted V again; every place that sets a frequency from code now goes through one function, so the next carry-over cannot bring it back
- The three places that answer "this needs your password" now answer the same way. Only one of them named whose account it was, and naming it is what lets the page ask for the password on the spot - so the same lock produced a prompt on a QTH and a refusal with a lecture on a note
