The other radios in America — FRS, GMRS, MURS and CB

Most two-way radios in the country are not amateur radios. These four services are 47 CFR Part 95 — the Personal Radio Services: no examination for any of them, no license at all for three, and channels rather than bands. An operator who can read the channel number on a child’s handheld, knows what the radio in the next truck is and thinks of channel 19 when the repeater does not answer is a better operator, and this is where that is learned.

Field reference — the NIFOG

The National Interoperability Field Operations Guide is CISA's pocket reference for incident communications, revised most years. It is where the standard interoperability channel names come from, and where a good many of the band charts in circulation were copied out of. For an amateur it holds the emergency and calling frequencies, the 60 m channels and why they exist, US bands and power limits, line-of-sight formulas, CTCSS and DCS tables, and the marine, aviation and weather listings you end up wanting to listen to.

Nearly nothing in it is amateur spectrum. Its channels need an authorisation an amateur license does not confer, and holding the guide is not that authorisation. Monitor freely; transmit only where you are licensed to.

Get it from CISA → Free, and free to copy — a work of the US government. There is an eNIFOG phone app that works with no signal.

Privileges are law; operating outside them is a violation. Activity segments are voluntary band plan convention with no legal force — but a signal in the wrong place is still the thing people complain about. Regional segments come from the local frequency coordinator, are fetched from their site rather than bundled with ELMER, and are cached for 30 days.
