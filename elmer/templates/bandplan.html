Field reference — the NIFOG

The National Interoperability Field Operations Guide is a pocket-sized technical reference for incident communications, published by CISA at the Department of Homeland Security and revised most years — version 2.02 is dated January 2025. It is the book the interoperability channel names and a good many of the band charts in circulation are copied out of, and it is strangely absent from the study material, so here it is.

Field Operations Guides at CISA → NIFOG page → The landing page outlives any one edition's file name. There is also an eNIFOG phone app that works with no signal, which is the form that is any use in the field.
What is in it for an amateur
  • Amateur emergency frequencies, and the Center of Activity calling frequencies — p. 103
  • The five 60 m channels, carrier and centre frequencies, and why they exist: federal stations are primary, amateurs secondary — p. 103
  • Amateur calling frequencies and the repeater coordinators by region — p. 105
  • US amateur bands and power limits — pp. 106 and 112
  • Line-of-sight formulas, CTCSS/NAC and DCS code tables — pp. 61 and 67
  • NOAA weather, marine, aviation, railroad and FRS/GMRS listings — the things you end up needing to listen to
  • Interference management, and how to file an STA
How it is actually used

It is a programming and planning document. The nationwide channels have standard names — VCALL10, VTAC11, UTAC41, 8TAC91 — and the point of the names is that everybody's radio calls the same frequency the same thing when several agencies turn up at the same incident. If you support a served agency, this is where the channel list and the tone codes for programming a radio come from, and it is what an ICS 205 communications plan is filled in against.

For most amateurs its everyday value is the other direction: knowing what a frequency is before you touch it, and knowing which of the thousands of channels in it are ones you may never transmit on.

Nearly nothing in the NIFOG is amateur spectrum. The interoperability, federal, marine and aviation channels it lists need an authorisation an amateur licence does not give you, and holding the book is not that authorisation. Even the amateur pages say it in as many words: a licence of the appropriate class is required, and those frequencies are not available to public safety agencies either. Part 97 does allow transmitting outside your privileges when there is an immediate threat to life or property and no other means — that provision is narrower than it sounds and is not a programming plan. Monitor freely; transmit only where you are licensed to.

A work of the US government, so it carries no copyright and you may print, copy and hand it out freely (17 U.S.C. §105) — with the ordinary caveat that a federal document can still contain third-party material that is marked as somebody's own, and this one does. ELMER links to it rather than bundling it, because it is revised and the copy on your shelf is the one that goes stale.

Privileges are law; operating outside them is a violation. Activity segments are voluntary band plan convention with no legal force — but a signal in the wrong place is still the thing people complain about. Regional segments come from the local frequency coordinator, are fetched from their site rather than bundled with ELMER, and are cached for 30 days.
