CW

Learn it, copy it, send it — and decode what is coming out of the receiver.
Characters are always sent at the character speed; lowering the effective speed stretches the gaps between them, not the characters themselves (Farnsworth). Learning a slowed-down character teaches a sound you have to unlearn later. Pick whatever tone you hear most comfortably — anywhere from 300 to 1200 Hz — and it is remembered.
The buttons are labelled the way a contact would put it, and each keys its code before it acts: QRV ready, go ahead · QRS send slower · QRQ send faster · QSM? please repeat · QSL received, understood · QRT stop sending. Soon enough you will think QRS when you feel rushed — and that is the code learnt.

Today
    press the key for what you hear - straight away

    Fifteen minutes a day beats two hours on Sunday. Every character is sent at full speed from the first day; what is slowed is the gap between them. A character is solid at nine in ten over twenty sends, and the next one arrives on its own. Type what you hear with your fingers on the keys; after each key a chime or a buzz, then the character that was sent, by name — the code and its name together, and never a mistake's.

    Koch method
    two characters at full speed, then one more at a time

    Start with two characters sent at the speed you eventually want to copy. When you are copying them at 90% or better, add the next. It feels brutal for a day and then it works, because you are learning the sound of each character rather than counting dots.

    Press below and each character is drawn as it sounds, then named.
    The shape is drawn while the character sounds and the letter appears two seconds later — the sound first, the name second, which is the order you want them learnt in. Turn the naming off once you no longer need it.
    Where you stand
    Green is copied reliably, amber is shaky, red needs work, grey is not met yet. Hover for the confusions — which character you actually heard it as — and how often it had to be sent again before it was copied.
    The whole code
    click anything to hear it

    Dits and dahs drawn to length rather than printed as dots and dashes: a dah is three times a dit, and the gap inside a character is one dit, which is what the spacing here shows. Prosigns are run together with no gap inside — that is the whole difference between the prosign AR and the letters A R, and it is audible.

    Copy practice

    Type what you hear. Nothing is revealed until you check. Ask for a resend as often as you need — a contact would — and the record keeps the count beside the copy.

    Your rating

    Two numbers, and where they stand: the speed you copy at, and the speed you send at. Each is the top rung of a ladder you passed - nine in ten characters right at that speed - and the games set their level from them.

    Copying

    Five groups of five come at a speed. Type what you heard and check. Nine in ten right and the next rung is two words a minute faster; short of that, two slower. The ladder settles in a few rungs.

    Sending

    On the Your sending pane, press Rate my sending: a line is shown, you key it - straight key or paddle, on the keys bound there or a paddle wired to them - and the decoder scores what it heard against what was asked. Your speed is read from your own dits on a straight key, and from the keyer's setting on a paddle.

    Send text

    Type anything and hear it as code, at the speed set above. Prosigns go in angle brackets — <AR>, <SK>, <BT> — and are sent run together, as one sound.

    Your sending

    Click either key to change it, then press the one you want. Both are remembered. A keyboard is a poor paddle and it is worth knowing why: most of them cannot report two arbitrary keys held at once, which is exactly what a squeeze is, so iambic sending can simply fail to register on some hardware. The modifier keys are wired separately and do not have that problem — left and right Ctrl, or the two Shifts, are the pair most likely to work. The levers above take a mouse, and two fingers on a touchscreen.

    Decode off air

    Point a microphone at the receiver's speaker. ELMER locks onto the loudest tone in the CW range and decodes the timing. It works on a clean, steady signal; QRM, QSB and a swinging fist all degrade it, exactly as they do for every other decoder. It works out the sending speed from what it hears, so expect the first character or two to garble while it finds the speed — then it locks on.
