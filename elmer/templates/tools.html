VNA — what the instrument sees, and how to trust it

A NanoVNA costs about what a cheap handheld does and has done more for amateur antenna work than anything else of the last decade, because it answers the question an SWR meter cannot. An SWR meter says how bad. A VNA says which way to cut — it measures the reactance with its sign, and the sign is the instruction.

The real instrument
Drive it

Sweeping asks the instrument a question and nothing is worse for having asked. These change what it is set to, and two of them destroy work — those say so and wait. Everything sent and everything answered is written down underneath, because most of these commands accept in silence, and the screen on the instrument itself is the other half of the check.

Calibrate it, one standard at a time

Set the span first — a calibration belongs to the sweep it was taken over. Then put each standard on the end of the same jumper that will screw onto the antenna, and press its button. Each one takes a sweep, so give it a moment. OPEN, SHORT and LOAD are all an antenna needs; ISOLN and THRU are for two-port work.

Calibrate it first — and calibrate it in the right place

A VNA measures what comes back. Before it can tell you what came back from the antenna, it has to be told what comes back from things whose answer is already known. That is calibration, it takes ninety seconds, and skipping it is why most first sweeps are wrong in a way that looks completely convincing.

  1. Set the span first. A calibration belongs to the sweep it was taken over. Change the start or stop afterwards and it is no longer valid — the instrument will interpolate and will not warn you.
  2. OPEN. Nothing on the port. All the power comes back, in phase. This teaches it the right-hand edge of the Smith chart.
  3. SHORT. The shorting cap on. All the power comes back, out of phase. The left-hand edge.
  4. LOAD. The 50 ohm cap on. Nothing should come back at all. The centre — and the one of the three whose quality decides how deep a null the instrument can honestly show.
  5. ISOLN and THRU if the menu asks: they are for two-port measurements. For an antenna you only need the three above.
  6. SAVE to a slot. A calibration that is not saved is gone the moment it is powered off, and you will not notice.

Calibrate at the far end of the coax you will use. Put the three standards on the end of the same jumper that will screw onto the antenna, not on the instrument's own port. Otherwise you have measured a beautiful antenna plus fifty feet of RG-58, and the coax will flatter it — that is the dashed trace above, and it is not an improvement, it is loss.

What a bad calibration looks like. A trace that never gets below about 1.3:1 anywhere, or a return loss that flattens out around 20 dB across the whole sweep, usually means the LOAD step was poor or the calibration is being interpolated onto a span it was not taken over. A trace with fine ripple on it that moves when you wiggle the cable is a connector, not an antenna. And a dip that shifts when you touch the coax is common-mode current on the outside of the braid, which a choke at the feedpoint fixes and no amount of trimming will.

Then check yourself against physics. Sweep a known half wave and see the dip land where the length says it should. An instrument that agrees with arithmetic you did beforehand is an instrument you can start to believe on an antenna you have never measured.

Sextant — where you are, from the sun and a clock

A sextant measures how high the sun is. That one number puts you somewhere on a circle drawn on the earth: every point on it sees the sun at that altitude at that instant. One sight is a circle. Two sights an hour apart are two circles, and they cross. That crossing is where you are, and everything below is arithmetic in service of it.

Navigators did this with an almanac, log tables and a plotting sheet, because the arithmetic was the expensive part. It is not expensive any more, so ELMER works out the sun's true altitude for any place and instant and searches for the position that best predicts every sight you took. No assumed position, no tables. It needs the time to be right — four seconds of clock error is a nautical mile of longitude, which is what WWV and CHU are broadcast for.

What one looks like, and what you see through it
The instrument
Sixty degrees of frame, which is where the name comes from, and every part on it in service of reading one angle.
Why it works
The part nobody guesses: you are not looking at the sun through it. You are looking straight ahead at the horizon through a piece of clear glass, and the sun has been folded down onto it by two mirrors. Both reach the same eye at the same instant, which is what makes the comparison possible from a moving deck.
Taking the sight
Never held one? What a sextant is, and how to take a sight

A sextant measures the angle between two things you can see at once. It does it with two mirrors: one on a swinging arm that catches the sun, one fixed and half silvered, so that looking through the eyepiece you see the horizon through the clear half and the sun reflected in the silvered half, in the same view at the same moment. Swing the arm until the sun sits on the horizon and read the angle off the arc. Everything else on the instrument — the micrometer drum for the last degree, the vernier for the last minute, the shades — is in service of reading that one angle finely.

The shades go on first, every time. The instrument concentrates the sun into your eye and there is no pain to warn you. Use the darkest shade that still shows the sun as a clean disc, and check before the sextant comes up to your face rather than after. Damage from this is immediate and permanent. If a shade is missing or cracked, that sextant is for looking at, not through.

Find the index error before you take anything. Set the arc to exactly zero and look at the horizon, or at anything far away and straight-edged. The direct and reflected images should form one unbroken line. If they are offset, the instrument's zero is not zero: bring them into line with the micrometer and read what it says. That figure, in arcminutes, goes in the box above, and it is the difference between a fix and a fix that is quietly a mile out. It is worth re-checking each session — the error moves with knocks and with temperature.

Taking a sight against a sea horizon. Set the arc near zero, put the shades in, and look at the sun. Now swing the index arm slowly and the sun will come down the field of view; follow it until it is near the horizon. Then rock the instrument gently side to side. The sun swings through an arc, and the bottom of that swing is the moment the sextant is truly vertical — that is the reading you want, and a sight taken without rocking is always too high. Turn the micrometer until the sun's lower edge just kisses the horizon, and call the instant you are satisfied. Read the arc afterwards; it will not move.

Inland, with an artificial horizon. There is no true horizon, so you make one: a pan of water, or old oil for a steadier surface, set somewhere out of the wind and shaded from direct sun. Instead of bringing the sun to a horizon you bring the real sun down to its own reflection in the pan until the two discs touch, edge to edge. Because you are measuring from the sun to its mirror image, the arc reads twice the altitude — type in what the instrument says and let ELMER halve it, which is what the horizon setting above is for. Do not halve it yourself, or the fix will come out on the wrong circle entirely. There is no dip to allow for, because you are not looking at a horizon at all, so the height-of-eye box goes quiet.

The clock matters more than the arc. Four seconds of error is a nautical mile of longitude, and a minute of arc is only a mile too — so a sloppy time ruins a careful sight. Two people is the easy way: one calls "mark" at the instant of contact and the other reads the watch. Alone, get the contact, hold it, and glance at the second hand. Set the watch against WWV on 5, 10, 15 or 20 MHz, or CHU on 3.330, 7.850 or 14.670, before you start and again after.

How many, and how far apart. Three or four, spread across an hour or more. Two sights taken minutes apart give circles that cross at a shallow angle, and a shallow crossing slides a long way for a small error. Take them, write them all down, and let the arithmetic decide — do not throw one away because you dislike it. If one really is wild, the working below will say so more honestly than your memory of taking it.

What to expect. A metal sextant in a practised hand is good to a mile or so. A plastic one — a Davis is the usual first sextant, and costs about what a cheap handheld does — is good to a few miles, which is a four-character grid square correct every time and a six-character one usually. Neither is a GPS, and that is not the point: the point is that it works with no satellite, no network and no permission from anybody, using a sky that cannot be jammed. The first few fixes will be bad. The tenth will not be.

Inland there is no sea horizon, so the usual answer is an artificial one — a shallow pan of water or old oil, sheltered from wind. You bring the real sun down to its reflection, which is twice the altitude, and there is no dip to allow for because you are not looking at a horizon at all.
No sextant at all: stand a stick up plumb, measure it, and measure its shadow. The altitude is atan(height / shadow). Read the middle of the soft edge — the sun is half a degree wide, so the shadow's edge is too. Half a centimetre on a metre of stick is about a fifth of a degree, which is loose for a sextant and ample for a four-character grid. It is most accurate with the sun low and its shadow long, which is the opposite way round from a sextant, so take these in the morning and the evening rather than at noon.

Two at least, three is better, and spread them across an hour or more — sights taken minutes apart cross at a shallow angle and the fix slides a long way for a small error.

Waves you can hear — why big ones get around and small ones do not

You already know this one. Two streets away you hear the bass from a car and none of the tune: the long waves bend around the houses and the short ones are stopped by them. Radio is the same game in a different medium, and mostly the same equations with a different constant — light goes 874,000 times faster than sound, so a radio wave and a sound wave of the same size are that far apart in pitch.

Drag the frequency. The wave is drawn to scale against the house, and the note is the sound of exactly that size in air — press it and listen.
